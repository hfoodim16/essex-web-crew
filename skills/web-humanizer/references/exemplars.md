# Exemplars — real website copy that reads like a person wrote it

Every line below is verbatim from a live site, attributed. They are here to be *read*
before drafting, not pasted. The value is in noticing what each one does structurally.

**Steal the structure. Never steal the voice.** Most of these brands are consumer brands
with permission to be funny. A transmission shop does not have that permission, and the
`voice-spec.md` register preset decides what does. Each entry says what transfers.

---

## Talking to the reader about their problem

> "Tell me if this sounds about right."
> — Basecamp, basecamp.com (section heading)

> "You're juggling people, projects, and expectations."
> — Basecamp

**What it does:** the heading is a sentence said to a person, and the subhead is the
reader's situation, not the product's features. No verb like "streamline" anywhere.
**Transfers to a trade site:** yes, directly. "Your transmission is slipping and two shops
told you two different things."

> "Software that's hard to use doesn't get used."
> — Basecamp

**What it does:** states a plain truth the reader already believes, which buys the next
line. Ten words, no adjective.
**Transfers:** yes. "A patio that holds water cracks by the second winter."

## Falsifiable specifics

> "Remember when companies cared about service? We still do."
> — Basecamp

> "In software, longevity like this isn't luck — it's proof it works."
> — Basecamp

**What it does:** anchors a claim to duration, something a reader can check.
**Transfers:** yes, with the em dash removed or replaced per copycheck's rate (trade-copy
allows ≤1 per 100 words; the period is usually better anyway).

> "Worn by supermodels in London and dads in Ohio."
> — New Balance (cited in Harry Dry's copywriting guide)

**What it does:** the whole positioning in nine words, entirely through two concrete
images. Nothing abstract. Passes the competitor-paste test — no other shoe brand could say
it.
**Transfers:** the technique does. "Booked by two country clubs and the guy across the
street."

> "It all started with 50 hours on a toilet and a dream."
> — Who Gives A Crap, us.whogivesacrap.org/pages/about-us

**What it does:** an origin story with a number in it. The number is what makes it read
true.
**Transfers:** the number does. The joke does not, on a trade site.

## Service scope, stated plainly

> "If you can drink it and it's got fruit and veg in it, we probably make it."
> — Innocent Drinks, innocentdrinks.co.uk

**What it does:** describes the whole product line with a rule instead of a list, and
"probably" is what makes it sound like a person.
**Transfers:** the shape transfers and it is genuinely useful for trades. "If it runs
water and it's in your house, we work on it." **The whimsy does not transfer** — Innocent's
lowercase-and-jokes register is a Tier 1B violation on a contractor's site. Read this one
for the sentence shape only.

> "We provide plumbing services across Birmingham, including Sutton Coldfield, Solihull,
> and Edgbaston (B1–B31)."
> — trade-site service-area pattern

**What it does:** the service area as a checkable list, with the postcode range. Zero
adjectives. This is what "for today's busy homeowners" should always have been.
**Transfers:** directly. Swap in the Essex County towns.

## Short CTAs

> "Buy the game." / "Steal the game."
> — Cards Against Humanity, cardsagainsthumanity.com

**What it does:** three words, a verb the customer would use, and no invented urgency.
**Transfers:** the plainness transfers. "Get a quote." "Call the shop." The provocation
does not.

> "Stay up to date on the latest news, promos and fart jokes."
> — Who Gives A Crap (newsletter CTA)

**What it does:** says exactly what you get, including the unflattering part. Honesty is
the mechanism, not the joke.
**Transfers:** the specificity. "One email in spring, one in fall, about scheduling."

## Register and confidence

> "Service isn't an afterthought, it's one of Basecamp's best features."
> — Basecamp

> "All these questions have the same answer: Yes!"
> — Basecamp

**What it does:** takes a position. Harry Dry's rule: fence-sitters don't buy.
**Transfers:** yes. "We don't do sealcoating. Ask us why."

> "Our foam isn't memory foam—it's even better."
> — Tuft & Needle, tuftandneedle.com

**What it does:** names the thing it is not, which is how customers actually think about a
category.
**Transfers:** yes, and it is strong on trade sites. "This isn't a patch. It's the whole
line replaced."

> "(because ramen spills are inevitable)"
> — Tuft & Needle (parenthetical inside an offer)

**What it does:** an aside that concedes something. Concession reads human because
marketing copy never concedes.
**Transfers:** carefully. On a trade site the concession is about the work, not a joke:
"(most driveways need the base redone, which is the part nobody quotes)".

> "Look mum, certifications!"
> — Who Gives A Crap (trust-section heading)

**What it does:** the *placement* is right — certifications get their own labeled block.
**Do not transfer the wording.** On a contractor's site that heading is "Licensed and
insured" with the numbers under it.

## The register benchmark

> "It's always more important to be clear than entertaining."
> — Mailchimp voice-and-tone guide, styleguide.mailchimp.com/voice-and-tone

> "We're weird but not inappropriate, smart but not snobbish."
> — Mailchimp

The second line is a register spec, and it is the *brand's* spec, not a universal one. Every
client gets their own, and it lives in `voice-spec.md`. Mailchimp's answer is Mailchimp's.
The first line is universal: **clear beats entertaining, every time, on every site this
crew builds.**

## Using this file

Before drafting a page, read the three entries matching what you are about to write (hero,
service card, CTA). Ask what each does structurally, then do that with the client's facts.

If you finish a line and cannot say which technique above it uses, it is probably a
template line. Check it against `tells.md`.
