Tree health

Emerald Ash Borer: know the symptoms.

The emerald ash borer is to blame for the death of millions of ash trees in 27 states — becoming a particularly prevalent concern in New Jersey. Native to Asia, it is an insect pest that infects and kills ash trees. It was first discovered in the United States in June 2002, and was likely unknowingly introduced ten years before. While white and black ash are particularly susceptible, all ash trees are at risk.

The adult beetle is about a half-inch long and an eighth-inch wide, with a metallic green body and a copper-red underbelly. The larvae are white or cream colored with an inch-long body made of ten bell-shaped abdominal segments.

Adults feed on the margins of the ash leaf, and the female lays eggs under the bark flaps on the trunk. The larvae tunnel further into the bark, feeding on the cambium — the layer of the tree that transports nutrients and water. Adult beetles surface in May or early June, leaving a three-to-four-millimetre hole shaped like a capital D, and feed on ash leaves until August. The full lifecycle takes a year. Once infested, trees will only live about three to four years.

Signs & symptoms

  • Woodpecker damage and heightened activity. Woodpeckers feed on the larvae living under the bark, particularly higher in the tree where these pests attack first. The damage looks like strips of bark peeled from the tree, called “flecking.”
  • Crown dieback. The result of years of feeding — dead branches, leaf loss, discolouration and thinning beginning at the top of the tree.
  • Bark splits. Vertical splits caused by callus tissue developing around the larval galleries; these often reveal the galleries beneath.
  • D-shaped exit holes. More often found higher in the canopy, but appearing lower on the trunk as the infestation progresses.
  • Epicormic branching. Branches and leaves sprouting wherever possible — typically below where the larvae are feeding, often at the base of the trunk.
  • S-shaped larval galleries. Winding tube designs under the bark, full of frass and sawdust.

Is it an ash tree?

  • Branches and buds sit directly across from each other, not staggered.
  • Compound leaves with 5–11 leaflets.
  • Tight bark with diamond-shaped ridges on mature trees; smooth bark on young ones.
  • Seeds are dry, oar-shaped samaras, usually in clusters, typically hanging on the tree until late fall or early winter.

What you can do

  • Identify and monitor the ash trees on your property.
  • Use traps to detect the insects.
  • Report sightings.
  • Ash trees can be treated to prevent infestation. Contact Happy Trees today for assistance in your ash tree protection.
